Tides of the North Sea

The North Sea has two high tides a day, and their height changes with the phase of the moon. Fishermen along the coast have kept tide tables for centuries, and the oldest surviving one, kept in a harbour office, dates from 1658.

Spring tides come shortly after a new or a full moon, when the sun and the moon pull in line. Neap tides come in between, and the difference between the two can reach two metres in the narrow estuaries of the eastern shore.

Reading a tide table

A tide table lists the time and the height of each high and low water. Read the guide to tide tables before your first crossing, and check the live readings on the morning itself, because wind and pressure move the sea by tens of centimetres.

Harbour offices still publish the table each year, and most of them now also print the corrections for summer time, which caught out many a visitor in the past.

def height(hours_after_low):
    return 1.2 + 2.4 * hours_after_low / 6

The formula above is the rule of twelfths in its simplest form, good enough to plan a walk across the flats but not to navigate by.
